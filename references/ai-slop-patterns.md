# AI slop pattern inventory

Use this as a diagnostic checklist. Look for clusters of predictable choices rather than isolated words.

## 1. Lexical tells

### Excess vocabulary

Watch for words that inflate simple claims: delve, intricate, meticulous, realm, underscore, boast, swift, pivotal, crucial, robust, seamless, comprehensive, transformative, groundbreaking, dynamic, multifaceted, nuanced, vital, innovative, cutting-edge, game-changing, invaluable, thought-provoking.

Fix by choosing the ordinary word unless the fancy word adds precision.

Examples:
- utilize -> use
- leverage -> use, build on, take advantage of
- facilitate -> help, make easier, run
- underscore -> show, make clear
- realm/landscape/ecosystem -> field, market, topic, group, system, product area
- robust -> strong, reliable, durable, broad, depending on meaning

Do not turn this into a word ban. A flagged word can be exact in context. Edit the sentence when the word is part of an inflated cluster or hides the real actor, action, or evidence.

### Abstract noun stacking

AI drafts often turn actions into noun piles: advancement, capability, implementation, optimization, transformation, enhancement, integration, utilization, significance, complexity, consideration, framework.

Fix by restoring actors and verbs.

Weak: "The implementation of the optimization framework enables enhanced collaboration."
Better: "The new workflow helps the sales and support teams update the same account record."

### Magic adverbs and vague intensifiers

Common markers: quietly, deeply, fundamentally, arguably, remarkably, significantly, effectively, strategically, seamlessly, thoughtfully, notably, importantly, primarily, ultimately.

Fix by proving the point or deleting the modifier.

## 2. Sentence and paragraph templates

### Negative parallelism

Patterns:
- "It is not X, it is Y."
- "Not just X, but Y."
- "The question is not X. The question is Y."
- "Not because X, but because Y."

Fix by stating the claim directly. Keep the contrast only when the reframe is genuinely surprising.

### Colon reveals

Pattern: a dramatic label followed by a colon and a reveal, such as “The best part: it learns” or “The real problem: nobody owns it.” This construction often performs suspense instead of building a sentence.

Fix by writing the claim as a normal sentence. Keep colons for real lists, labels, quotations, and explanations where the punctuation clarifies the relationship.

### Superficial analysis clauses

Pattern: a factual clause followed by a trailing `-ing` phrase such as “highlighting,” “underscoring,” “reflecting,” or “showcasing” that announces significance without proving it.

Fix by naming the supported consequence or deleting the interpretive tail. Do not replace it with a different importance claim.

### Inflated proxy verbs

Patterns: “serves as,” “stands as,” “offers,” “provides,” or “boasts” used to avoid a clearer `is`, `has`, or concrete action.

Fix by using the plain linking verb or naming what the subject actually does. Keep the original verb when it carries a real distinction.

### Synonym cycling

Pattern: one entity becomes “the agent,” then “the assistant,” then “the tool” solely to avoid repetition.

Fix by repeating the precise term. Elegant variation is harmful when it blurs identity, ownership, or sequence.

### Dramatic countdowns

Pattern: "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."

Fix by writing the sentence a person would actually say.

### Rhetorical Q/A punchlines

Patterns:
- "The result?"
- "The problem?"
- "The worst part?"
- "The takeaway?"

Fix by folding the answer into the sentence or using a normal transition.

### False range constructions

Pattern: "From X to Y" where X and Y are not a real spectrum.

Weak: "From innovation to implementation, teams need alignment."
Better: "Teams need alignment during design, rollout, and support."

### Listicle in disguise

Patterns:
- "The first... The second... The third..."
- Identical paragraph starts.
- Numbered structure hidden in prose.

Fix by using an actual list when a list helps, or combine points into a real argument.

### Fractal summaries

Pattern: intro summary, section summary, conclusion summary, and micro-summary after every point.

Fix by cutting all summary layers except the one the reader needs.

### Manufactured endings

Patterns:
- a conclusion paragraph that restates the draft
- “Ultimately,” “Overall,” or “In conclusion” followed by recap
- a final short line that turns the point into an aphorism, cute metaphor, or mic drop

Fix by ending on the last concrete point, supported takeaway, or next action. Delete a fake-profound kicker instead of polishing it into a better slogan.

## 3. Tone tells

### Teacher mode

Patterns: "Let's break this down," "Let's unpack," "Let's dive in," "It is important to understand," "Think of it as."

Fix by addressing the reader as competent. Explain only what must be explained.

### False suspense

Patterns: "Here's the thing," "Here's the kicker," "Here's what most people miss," "This is where it gets interesting."

Fix by giving the point without stage directions.

### False vulnerability

Patterns: "I'll be honest," "Let's be real," "And yes," "This is not a rant; it's a diagnosis."

Fix by using a concrete admission or no admission at all.

### Manufactured intimacy

Patterns: "Can I be real for a second?" "Can we be honest?" "Real talk." These lines announce authenticity instead of saying the specific thing.

Fix by opening on the admission, consequence, or stakes. Do not replace the phrase with a different permission-to-confess formula.

### Self-interview

Pattern: the writer asks "Honestly?" "Sound familiar?" "Crazy, right?" or another question only to answer it immediately.

Fix by connecting the thought directly. Keep a real question hook, a question addressed to another person, or a question that allows an actual pause or response.

### Grandiose stakes

Patterns: "This will define the next era," "fundamentally reshape," "change everything," "revolutionize the way we think."

Fix by naming the actual consequence and scale.

### Vague authority

Patterns: "Experts say," "Industry reports suggest," "Observers argue," "Many believe."

Fix by naming the source or cutting the claim.

## 4. Formatting tells

### Markdown over-formatting

Signals: every bullet starts with a bold label, decorative bold inside sentences, too many headings, headings over tiny sections, emoji bullets, checkmark-heavy lists, ornamental horizontal rules, a list where two sentences would read better, or a table used for simple prose.

Fix by using plain paragraphs or sparse bullets.

### Em-dash addiction

AI uses em dashes for drama, pivots, and asides. A few are fine; many create a synthetic rhythm.

Fix by replacing most with commas, parentheses, colons, semicolons, or separate sentences.

### Unicode decoration

Signals: arrows, smart quote overuse, unusual bullets, decorative icons, emoji used as section markers.

Fix by using ordinary punctuation unless the platform expects otherwise.

## 5. Content-level tells

### Generic examples

Weak: "For example, businesses can improve efficiency and customer satisfaction."
Better: "A clinic could cut duplicate intake calls by letting patients update insurance details before the appointment."

### No real constraint

AI drafts often describe a clean world with no budget, deadline, tradeoff, confused stakeholder, legacy system, or exception.

Fix by adding a true constraint from the context or leaving the sentence modest.

### Over-balanced caveats

Pattern: "While X has benefits, it is important to consider Y."

Fix by making the tradeoff specific.

Weak: "While automation improves efficiency, it is important to consider human oversight."
Better: "Automation helps with repeat tickets, but billing disputes still need a person who can read the account history."

### Unowned claims

AI prose often has no authorial stance: it sounds correct, balanced, and empty.

Fix by making the stance visible: what matters, what changed, what the writer recommends, what they reject, and why.

## 6. Rhythm tells

### Low burstiness

AI prose often has similar sentence lengths and a clean topic-sentence cadence. Human prose usually has more rhythm variation: a short sentence after a dense one, a longer sentence when the idea needs room, a fragment only when the voice supports it.

Fix by editing for thought rhythm, not random variation.

### Repeated sentence starts

Patterns: "This means... This allows... This creates..." or "They can... They can... They can..."

Fix by combining sentences or changing the logic order.

### Over-smoothing

AI rewrites remove friction: hedges, asides, plain admissions, local vocabulary, and specific annoyance. Do not sand away every quirk. Keep the human parts that help the piece.

Over-smoothing can also make every paragraph equally tidy. Leave a strong human sentence alone. Preserve bluntness, humor, profanity, self-interruption, and deliberate fragments when they belong to the writer and remain clear.

### Page voice pasted into speech

A sentence can be clear on the page and still catch in the mouth. Warning signs include long nested clauses, evenly spaced breaths, formal transitions, a pristine call to action, and dialogue that sounds like a brochure.

Fix with the spoken-delivery pass. Preserve the speaker's real contractions, repetitions, fragments, and asides, but do not insert generic filler or fake stumbles.

### Minted modifier chains

AI drafts can coin hyphenated modifiers to simulate precision: several ordinary words compressed into a label nobody in the source actually uses.

Fix by unpacking the label into the plain claim. Keep established terms of art, product names, and compounds that appear in the user's field or samples.

## 7. Research-informed cues

Stylometric work often examines lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, contractions, punctuation, named entities, sentiment, readability, and n-gram uniqueness. Other studies have found LLM-assisted texts can show lower lexical diversity, longer or more complex words, and excess vocabulary such as delve, underscore, meticulous, intricate, realm, pivotal, and related terms.

Use these cues to edit for naturalness, clarity, specificity, and voice.
